AWS Messaging

AWS Communications tenets

  1. Customer Trust

    • Part of the way we build and maintain trust is by delivering a positive message

  2. Customer Obsession

    • We’re not going to participate in press stories or events that don’t allow us to focus on the customer’s story

  3. Honesty and Authority

    • We avoid spin and speculation, stick to the facts, and take time to prepare thoroughly before presenting on potentially difficult situations.

  4. Quality over quantity

    • We care more about the quality of the communications than the quantity. We’ll turn down opportunities if we don’t think the results are going to be positive for AWS or valuable for our customers.

AWS Core messaging

  1. Responses to questions

    • **The AWS Messaging FAQ: Core Messaging document** and the AWS Messaging FAQ: Global Infrastructure, Security, Privacy & Compliance document serve as a guide for review and study. Pay close attention to the exact messaging, including the order in which the messaging is written. This is how AWS CEO Andy Jassy answers questions publicly, and he has put a great deal of thought into the best way to discuss each topic.

    Quotes from Andy : **This company is built on our customers. We’re focused on what will benefit them in both our work and in our communications**.

    Quotes from Andy : We’ve grown quickly because of our track record. Security and operational excellence are our top priorities.

    Quotes from Andy : **We have a relentless focus on innovation. This is both from the perspective of quickly bringing new services and features to market, and by investing significantly in finding hardware, software, and operational efficiencies so that we can pass our own cost savings along to customers in the form of price drops.**

  2. Statistics

    • Our public stats are approved and updated regularly. Be sure to check the Public Stats Wiki prior to your speaking events to ensure accuracy.

  3. Customer stories

    • Customer stories help engage the audience and showcase customer benefits. Stories are especially helpful for Q&As to help you redirect tough questions. When speaking about customers, be sure to cover:

      • Who they are.

      • How the customer uses AWS.

      • The benefit the customer is getting out of using AWS.

    • For the latest information about customer stories, go to the AWS Top-Level Messaging page on Wisdom and scroll down to the AWS Customers section. You’ll find regional Top Customer References documents that are updated on a quarterly basis.

  4. Analyst quotes

    • At times, it might be beneficial to cite analyst reports to augment your messaging.

    • Contact the Analyst Relations teams for the latest information.

Techniques when delivering AWS messaging

💡

**Use approved AWS core messaging**

Tips to deliver on-message answers to questions

  1. **Pause after a question** to buy yourself time to think, “What messaging applies here?” You can do this by drinking a sip of water or repeating the question to the audience.

  2. **Listen for keywords** to help you decide on the messaging that applies.

  3. Listen to the entire question to ensure that you know **exactly what is being asked**.

  4. Don’t be afraid to **ask clarifying questions** if you aren’t sure that you understand the question.

Address the specific question

  • Pay attention to the question you are asked, and stick to the messaging for that topic area.

  • Ensure that you answer the question that was asked.

  • Don’t go beyond the scope of the question because it could open up topics that had not yet been explored.

  • If you get stuck, think back to the global communications tenets—customer trust, customer obsession, honesty and authority, and quality over quantity—and then work your way back to that topic.

  • Ask for further clarification if you need more time to recall the best response.

  • Using a canned response that doesn’t feel like it addresses what was asked does not build trust with our customers. Instead, do the following:

    1. Bring it **back to our core AWS messaging** somewhere in your response. What can you say that connects their question to our messaging?

    2. **Don’t wrap up** your response with, "**Did I answer your question?**" Doing so is likely to elicit a follow-up question even if they feel they got the answer.

    3. Don’t be afraid to say, “**I’m not the best person to answer** that question” if you are not the expert on that topic or you know that it is information you should not discuss publicly.

Do and Don’ts

  1. Do

    • Provide visuals, if appropriate, to emphasize your points.

    • Prepare and practice.

    • Anticipate how you will answer tough questions by **practicing bridging techniques**.

    💡

    bridging : a technique used at AWS to move from a difficult question to a prepared key message

    • Check Wisdom for the latest approved content, such as customer references and hot topics.

    • Freshen up your customer and partner examples.

    • Be prepared to close with a final thought or have an answer to the question, ”Anything else you want to share?”

  2. Don’t

    • Don’t discuss competitors.

    • Don’t use boastful phrases.

    • Don’t use negative language that could result in a half quote.

    • Don’t say or infer that AWS is a public cloud.

    • Don’t say or infer that customers should use multiple clouds.

    • Don’t say or infer that AWS is an infrastructure company like software as a service (SaaS).

    • Don’t talk about anyone you don’t want a reporter to call, such as customers, partners, or competitors.

    • Don’t say Amazon when you really mean AWS‌.

    • Don’t use shortened versions of service names like “EC2” or “S3”—use “Amazon EC2” and “Amazon S3.”

    • Don’t use third-party statistics or data that’s not approved.

    • Don’t disclose any information you wouldn’t want AWS competitors to know.

    • Don’t state how many employees work on a project, in a team, or in a division.

    • Don’t assume customers are keeping up with the AWS pace of innovation.

    • Don’t say “I” when you really mean “AWS.”

    • Don’t offer additional interviews or agree to any other engagement.

Techniques when responding to questions

  1. Control the Q&A

    • When you are on stage, you can control the direction and tone of the Q&A session. The way you respond can dictate the next question that someone asks. Don’t invite unnecessary questions, and avoid ending your response with questions like, “Does that make sense?” or “Did I answer your question?” This will prompt another question, even if you did answer the initial question and it made sense.

  2. Handling difficult questions

    • There’s no way that we can prepare you for every question you might be asked, and there might be times when someone in the audience asks you a particularly difficult question. The best you can do is prepare for these difficult questions so that you’re more comfortable when you respond. Respond with the right attitude, in a professional manner, and with an answer that aligns with the AWS core messaging and communications tenets. Being humble, approachable, smart, and respectful wins the trust of the inquirer and others in the audience. It also demonstrates that you want to answer their question to the best of your abilities.

    • If you don’t know the answer to the question, it’s okay to say “I am not the right person to answer that question,” and then refer them to PR. It’s better to say this than to accidentally give inaccurate or speculative information

  3. Art of the pushback

    • One of the most common mistakes that speakers make is answering negative questions without first disagreeing with the premise of that question. There are moments to push back and counteract that negative perception so that you don’t perpetuate it. We refer to this as “the art of the pushback.”

    • An example of how you might push back is by using one of the delivery techniques and saying “Actually, what we are hearing in our conversations with customers is…” and then bridge back to the AWS core messaging

  4. Responding to sensitive questions

    • As a speaker, you’ll also need to know when it’s appropriate not to respond. Generally, we don’t talk about:

      • Rumors or speculation: You don’t want to be seen as giving credibility to rumors or extending the press cycle, even if it’s true. This will likely never be your problem to fix.

      • What’s under the hood: We consider our infrastructure and processes to be our own “secret sauce,” so we are careful about what we disclose publicly. If there is an interesting story or a compelling customer reason to do so, we will decide when to share a little bit more (as we did with how Amazon Lex, AWS CodeDeploy, and Amazon DynamoDB came to be), but these are carefully reviewed by senior leadership ahead of time.

      • The size of AWS teams, projects, or organizations: Our competitors may use this information against us because we generally have small, lean teams that are expected to accomplish a lot compared to what other companies have.

    • Again, it’s okay to say you don’t know and refer them to the PR team. Doing this can make you look more honest and humble, thereby building trust, and the PR team can give the approved answer. Do not try to guess the answer or use unapproved messaging when you’re not sure.

  5. Handling hot topics

    • Take the time to check the AWS Messaging FAQ: Hot Topics document prior to every speaking event. Even after practicing the current hot topics messaging, there can be a case when something happens on the day you speak and everyone wants you to talk about it. There might not be PR messaging yet to guide you. It might be a statement released by AWS or Amazon or even by a competitor. In some cases, it might have nothing to do with your presentation or area of expertise, and other times it might.

    • Take the distributed denial of service (DDoS) attacks in 2016 as an example. Audiences wanted answers and they brought their questions to speaking events, leaving our speakers in the hot seat. For events this significant, we’ll have a prepared statement, so make sure you are on the AWS Compete email distribution. You’ll receive AWS Field Alerts on hot issues in the press.

    • If you’re aware of hot topics that you are expecting to hear a question on, contact PR before the event so they can provide you with guidance. You can find the list of PR contacts in the Resources section.

    • If you find yourself in the hot seat, remember these guidelines:

      • Use your judgment based on the communications and messaging best practices.

      • Always refer back to the approved messaging

  6. Your parting thought

    • It’s always a good idea to be ready to summarize the conversation, reiterate key messages, or talk about a great customer example at the end of your presentation’s Q&A session. Be ready to sum it up as soon as you stop talking. Be prepared for someone to ask you, “Do you have any closing thoughts?” You can answer this question in any way you want—just make sure you end the conversation on a positive note!

Q&A sessions dos and don’ts

  1. Do :

    • Respect your interviewer.

    • Repeat key talking points.

    • Focus on the facts.

    • Prepare. Know your audience and the purpose of the event or show, and anticipate probable questions.

    • Rehearse sound bites and interview techniques.

  2. Don’t :

    • Don’t give an answer that you’re not confident is factually accurate or make claims that are not backed by good data.

    • Don’t say, “No comment.” This often has a negative connotation.

    • Don’t use filler words or say, “that’s a good question” after a question is asked.

    • Don’t invite new questions.

    • Don’t talk too fast, too slowly, or too quietly.

    • Don’t cross your arms or seem defensive.

    • Don’t use distracting mannerisms (such as adjusting clothing or pacing).

Legal considerations - don’t

  • Do not use terms like “ensure,” “guarantee,” “unlimited,” or “100 percent uptime,” and do not suggest that AWS will unilaterally make data secure, meet compliance standards, or ensure compliance.

  • Don’t state that AWS offers services with no commitments or contracts.

  • Don’t talk to press or analysts, including “off the record” or “on background,” without a member of the Communications team present.

  • Don’t comment on potential merger and acquisitions (M&A) activity.

  • Don’t comment on rumors or speculation.

  • Don’t disclose non-public information (financials, internal secret sauce, roadmap, etc.).

  • Don’t add commentary to any financial disclosures or earnings, including if past guidance has changed.

  • Don’t discuss securities offerings, buybacks, repurchases, etc.

  • Don’t use the term “free” without Legal team review.

Speaking policies and opportunities

Communications policies

  • Handling press or analyst outreach online

    • do not respond, and do not engage in a conversation. Instead, immediately forward the message to AWS PR or AR—don’t even say “thank you” or explain that you are passing it along

  • Handling press or analyst reach out in person

    • NEVER talk to a reporter or analyst in person without someone from the PR or AR teams by your side having coordinated the meeting or interview

    • Let’s say you are working a booth at an event, or you’ve just presented at a conference, and a reporter or an analyst comes up to you and asks a question. The correct way to handle this is to:

      1. Thank them for their interest.

      2. Ask for their card (but don’t give them your business card).

      3. Say you will have someone from the Communications team follow up with them because they will be the best person to help them.

  • Social media policies and requirements

    • Do not blog, tweet, or use other social media on behalf of AWS without completing the AWS Social Media Training and Certification test.

    • We also recommend reading the AWS Blog and following @awscloud, @jeffbarr, and @Werner on Twitter.

Knowing your audience

Customizing your presentation

  1. Define who the customer is.

  2. Describe how the customer uses AWS.

  3. Feature the benefits that the customer receives from using AWS.

  • Reference a diverse set of customers. Don’t limit to customers from only one line of business.

  • Source best-in-class customer references from the website, from your customer experiences, and from the customer references team

  • Verify you have up-to-date and approved information in your presentation. You can check the Speaker Info Wiki and resources links and the Analyst Data Repository to find commonly requested reports, approved slides, stats, and market data

  • Remember to convey honesty and confidence, use language from the AWS website when describing products and services, and talk with any co-presenter to make sure they also are on message. Use the guide. It covers this and much more.

  • Besides checking that the content is on message, the speaker quick guide also includes how best to communicate your message.

Creating Presentation Content

Content standards and best practices

  • Keep the title under 60 characters

    • ex) Preparing your development and engineering teams for cloud transformation

  • Use keywords to draw the audience in

    • ex) Inspection mechanisms : Using data and dash-boarding

  • Grab the reader’s interest

    • ex) How to successfully become a machine learning developer

  • Write for a global audience

    • Avoid using idioms, stereotypes, and culture-specific metaphors to be more inclusive of all audience members.

    • ex) Raise the bar : Helping enterprises transform using the cloud → Transform your enterprise using the cloud

Slide decks

  • Any slide deck that you use for a speaking event should be created in an approved AWS template. This ensures a consistent look and feel (or branding) for customer-facing AWS content. Events such as re:Invent, re:Inforce, summits, and others require event-specific templates that are designed to distinguish that event from the many other events that AWS hosts. If you are speaking for an AWS event, check with the team managing that event to see if there is a template you are required to use.

  • If you are creating a presentation that does not require a specific template, use one of the AWS templates available on the AWS Brand Portal. This will ensure that your presentation aligns with AWS branding guidelines (it uses the Amazon Ember font, it uses the correct AWS brand colors, etc.).

  • When building your slide deck, keep in mind that slides should enhance your message, not give your message for you. As the AWS speaker, you are the star of the show, not your slides.

  • Image Standards

    • Your visuals should relate to what you are saying. Otherwise, they could confuse the audience. Memes and funny cat photos definitely grab interest, but they can also distract from your actual message.

    • Avoid using images that are gimmicky or overly literal (e.g., a giant question mark for a questions slide or a picture of a hammer to convey the idea of AWS tools and services).

    • If an image or graphic is complex, don’t show everything. Instead, focus on the part of the image that is most important.

  • Customer Logos

    • If you want to use a customer logo in a presentation, check Amazon Reference Finder to confirm that the customer has given AWS permission to use their logo. Note that the information in this database is updated frequently, and our customers do sometimes change their minds. It’s best practice to verify all logos you’re using in a slide deck even if you’ve used them for a previous speaking engagement.

  • Partner logos

    • To determine if a company is an AWS partner, check the AWS Partner Solutions Finder.   • AWS logos

    • If you plan to use AWS logos, make sure to download the correct logos from the AWS Brand Portal.

  • Open-source logos

    • Logos that represent open-source projects or products, frameworks, protocols, and the like are often protected by trademark rights. It is best practice to check the logo usage guidelines or policies of the open-source organization in question.

  • AWS icons

    • AWS offers a public-facing toolkit of AWS Architecture Icons and an AWS Illustration Library of icons that you can add to your slides or use to create architecture diagrams

Legal guidelines and editing support

Copyright

Legal requirements

  • Ensure that any AWS service names are correct (follow the AWS Service Names guidelines).

  • Do not include or display personally identifiable information (PII) of a person (including photos, social media handles, phone numbers, email addresses, avatars, etc.) without their written permission.

  • As much as possible, fictitious content (people names, company names, IP addresses, user credentials, hashes, domains, buckets, etc.) should come from the AWS Safe Fictitious Names and Numbers list.

  • Do not disparage any companies or their products or services.

  • Do not invent new language to describe a feature or product that is already described on the AWS site. As much as possible, use the language from the AWS site, which is approved by Legal.

  • Make sure that any claim you make is verifiable and factually correct. Avoid using terms like “best,” “first,” “only,” “leading,” “never,” “only,” “always,” etc. unless you get permission from Legal to do so.

  • Do not give details about a customer use case unless you have permission from the customer (i.e., unless that customer is a referenceable customer; you can find referenceable customers in Reference Finder).

Final Checks

  • AWS service names are correct

  • Slides do not include PII

  • Fictitious content complies with legal requirements

  • Image permissions have been verified

  • All logos are used with permission

  • All claims are verifiable

  • All customers mentioned are referenceable customers

  • No content that disparages other companies is included (either stated or implied)

  • Spell check has been run

  • Presentation has been checked in Slide Show mode to ensure that it runs properly

  • Slides have been peer reviewed
